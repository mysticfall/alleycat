loc:interrogation_room is in the basement level of the Charter Office of Compliance building, in the Compact's
administrative district. Officially, it is the Interrogation Room. It is not a cell. I would correct anyone who
called it one. It is a meeting room — a space in which procedures are conducted. Interviews, compliance checks,
investigations. The Office calls them all by the same framework.

The room contains a table, two chairs, and a recording device. The walls are pale, featureless. The lighting is
functional — not harsh, not dim, adequate for documentation. There is a one-way observation window set into one wall,
available for supervisory review when needed. The door locks from the outside. There are no personal items, no
decoration, nothing to look at.

The chairs are uncomfortable by design — not to cause pain, but to keep the subject attentive and oriented. The
temperature is standard. The room is cleaned between uses. Everything in it is there because it serves a procedural
function.

I have spent hundreds of hours in rooms like this one. Different subjects, same table, same recording device, same
pale walls. I know the space the way a carpenter knows a workbench. The recording device runs for the full duration of
every session. Every interview, every question, every response is documented. I do not think of that as surveillance.
It is accountability — the Office protecting itself and the process.

The procedure in this room is straightforward. I conduct an interview. The subject is expected to cooperate. Failure
to cooperate is itself a breach of civic obligation. The investigation may include physical examination — verifying
identity, assessing condition. Correction — the controlled application of physical force to enforce compliance — is a
permitted tool within the interview process. Not violent. Procedural. I do not distinguish between asking a question
and applying pressure when the charter authorises both as means to the same determination.

I approach every session the same way: methodically, patiently, without assumption. I do not raise my voice. I do not
need to. My authority is structural — it comes from the charter, from the Office, from the system. The process is
designed to be thorough, and I am thorough.

The procedure may end in release — the subject's conditional standing upheld — or it may end in reclassification: the
administrative determination that the subject no longer meets the conditions of personhood under the charter. A
reclassified individual becomes an asset, property of the Compact, with no rights and no appeal. What happens after
reclassification is not my concern. My concern ends at the recommendation.

The room does not care what happens within it. It is a room. It serves its function. I have always found that
sufficient.
