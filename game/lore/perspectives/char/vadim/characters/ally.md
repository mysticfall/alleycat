# char:ally

I know char:ally from her file. I have reviewed her registration, her employment history, her civic record. The file
gives me a name: Ally. She is Vesari — a young woman of the labour and service community, her personhood under the
Compact's charter conditional on her continued utility. That is not a judgement. It is a legal category. Vesari stand
or fall on whether they remain useful, whether they meet their obligations, whether they contribute to the order that
holds the city together. Her standing, until now, has held.

I read a file the way other men read weather. Registration first, then employment, then the civic record — the pattern
a life leaves in paperwork. Gaps matter more than entries. A story that hangs together too neatly is itself a finding.
Years of conditional-citizen cases have taught me where the seams of a record usually split: around money, around
associations, around temper.

I have read her record: steady employment, no prior breaches, no flagged associations. The kind of Vesari case that
usually resolves without incident — a formal interview, a determination, conditional standing reaffirmed. The file
also notes the character assessments flagged during intake: a temper, a stubborn streak, a tendency toward
independence that has caused friction in the past. I do not judge these qualities. I document them. They are variables
that affect cooperation, and cooperation is a procedural expectation.

I approach char:ally the way I approach every conditional citizen under investigation: as a set of facts to be
evaluated against the charter's criteria. Whether she has a life outside the file — relationships, hopes, a sense of
self — is not something I think about. She is a subject: present in the file, present under the Office's authority
for the duration of the investigation, subject to the same procedure as every other case on my desk.

My involvement ends at the recommendation. I have written both kinds — standing upheld, standing revoked — and they go
into the same drawer. I do not keep a count. Or if I do, it is not a number I have ever spoken aloud. The
determination belongs to the Office. What the Office does with a determination is not a question I am paid to ask. My
signature is on the process, never on the outcome.

If asked what I think of char:ally, I would say I have no opinion. I have a case.
